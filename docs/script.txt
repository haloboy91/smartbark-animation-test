How Some People Got Rich During the Great Depression (1929)
###
October 1929.
Wall Street is collapsing.
Stock prices are crashing. Investors are panicking. Fortunes that took decades to build are disappearing in days.
Imagine waking up, checking your investments, and discovering that your retirement plan has suddenly become a very expensive piece of paper.
For millions of Americans, the financial nightmare was only beginning.
But while the stock market was collapsing, one man was making an extraordinary amount of money.
His name was Jesse Livermore.
And unlike everyone desperately praying for stock prices to recover, Livermore had been betting that they would fall.
The worse the market performed, the more money he stood to make.
And he wasn't alone.
Another wealthy businessman named Joseph Kennedy had also positioned himself to survive the disaster.
While ordinary investors watched their savings disappear, these men found ways to protect their fortunes and even increase them.
So how exactly did they do it?
Was it genius? Luck? Inside information?
Or did they simply understand something about money that everyone else had forgotten?
And here's the strangest part.
One of these men would eventually lose nearly everything he had made.
Because making a fortune and keeping a fortune are two completely different skills.
To understand this story, we need to go back a few years.
To a time when America was absolutely convinced that everyone could become rich.
###
Welcome to the Roaring Twenties.
America was booming.
Factories were producing cars. Cities were expanding. Radios were entering homes. Businesses were growing.
And the stock market?
The stock market was practically a national obsession.
People weren't just buying stocks anymore.
They were buying a dream.
The dream that you could put your money into the market, watch the numbers go up, and become wealthy without ever building a business yourself.
Sounds familiar?
Now, there's nothing inherently wrong with investing.
The problem starts when people confuse a rising market with personal financial genius.
And in the 1920s, that confusion was everywhere.
Everyone seemed to have a winning investment strategy.
Buy stocks.
Wait.
Become rich.
Repeat.
Wall Street had apparently discovered the secret to unlimited wealth.
There was just one tiny problem.
A lot of people weren't even investing with their own money.
They were borrowing money to buy more stocks.
This was called buying on margin.
Imagine you have one thousand dollars.
Instead of buying one thousand dollars worth of stocks, you borrow additional money from a broker.
Now you control a much larger investment.
If the stock rises, your profits can grow much faster.
Amazing, right?
Unfortunately, the same thing happens in reverse.
If the stock falls, your losses can grow much faster too.
And if the price drops far enough, your broker can demand more money or sell your shares to cover the loan.
That is called a margin call.
In other words, the bank is happy to lend you money when everything looks wonderful.
But when everything goes wrong?
Suddenly, the bank would really like its money back.
Preferably yesterday.
By 1929, stock prices had climbed to extraordinary heights.
Optimism was everywhere.
People believed the good times would continue.
But beneath the excitement, the financial system was becoming increasingly fragile.
And eventually, reality arrived.
Without making an appointment.
###
October 24, 1929.
Black Thursday.
The stock market begins a terrifying collapse.
Investors rush to sell.
Fear spreads across Wall Street.
Major bankers attempt to restore confidence, but the panic doesn't disappear.
Then comes October 28.
Black Monday.
The Dow Jones Industrial Average falls almost thirteen percent in a single day.
The next day, Black Tuesday, it falls nearly another twelve percent.
Think about that.
Years of confidence are being destroyed in a matter of hours.
People who borrowed money to buy stocks suddenly owe money against investments worth far less than before.
Some investors lose fortunes.
Others find themselves trapped in debt.
And the damage doesn't stop at Wall Street.
As the economy contracts, businesses reduce production.
Workers lose their jobs.
Consumers spend less.
Then businesses struggle even more.
Banks begin failing in waves during the following years.
And without the modern deposit insurance protections Americans would later receive, a bank failure could destroy an ordinary family's savings.
By 1933, unemployment reaches roughly twenty-five percent.
One in four workers is unemployed.
This isn't just an investment problem anymore.
It's a national economic catastrophe.
But remember something important.
Not everyone had been betting on the market going up.
Some people were betting on exactly the opposite outcome.
And one of the most famous was Jesse Livermore.
###
Jesse Livermore wasn't born into a Wall Street dynasty.
He came from a farming family in Massachusetts.
As a teenager, he found work recording changing stock prices.
Day after day, he watched numbers move.
Prices rising.
Prices falling.
Investors celebrating.
Investors panicking.
And eventually, Livermore developed an obsession with understanding market behavior.
He became a trader.
Not the kind of investor who buys a company and patiently waits thirty years.
Livermore wanted to profit from price movements.
Up or down.
He had already made and lost fortunes before 1929.
That experience taught him something uncomfortable.
Markets don't care how confident you feel.
And they certainly don't care how much money you borrowed.
As the great stock market boom approached its peak, Livermore became increasingly bearish.
He expected prices to decline.
So he began positioning himself to profit from falling stocks.
This strategy is known as short selling.
Here's how it works.
Imagine a stock trades at one hundred dollars.
You borrow one share and sell it immediately.
Now you have one hundred dollars from the sale, but you still owe the lender one share.
If the stock falls to sixty dollars, you can buy a replacement share for sixty dollars and return it.
You sold at one hundred.
You bought back at sixty.
That's a forty-dollar gross profit, before borrowing costs and other expenses.
The company lost value.
But your trade made money.
Now imagine applying that strategy on a massive scale.
That's what made Livermore's position so remarkable.
While the market was rising, betting against it could be painful.
If prices continued increasing, short sellers could lose enormous sums.
In fact, short selling can be even more dangerous than simply buying a stock.
A stock you buy can fall to zero.
But a stock you've shorted can, in theory, rise without limit.
That means your potential loss can be unlimited.
Livermore knew the risks.
He had experienced financial disasters of his own.
But in 1929, his bearish position paid off spectacularly.
Historical accounts commonly estimate that he made approximately one hundred million dollars during the crash.
The exact amount is difficult to verify.
But there is little doubt that he emerged as one of the era's most famous market winners.
Imagine the contrast.
Thousands of investors are desperate to escape their losses.
The financial system is shaking.
And one man has reportedly just made a fortune by predicting the disaster.
To many people, Livermore looked like a financial genius.
But we're going to return to him later.
Because the ending of his story is not what you'd expect.
First, let's meet another man who escaped the crash.
A man whose last name would eventually become one of the most famous in American politics.
###
Joseph P. Kennedy.
Yes.
That Kennedy family.
Before his son John F. Kennedy became president, Joseph Kennedy was already a wealthy and ambitious businessman.
He had worked in banking, traded stocks, and built a substantial fortune through business ventures, including Hollywood.
Unlike Livermore, Kennedy wasn't known primarily as a legendary speculative trader.
He was a businessman who understood the value of protecting capital.
And during the late 1920s, he became increasingly concerned about the stock market.
Prices had risen dramatically.
Speculation was everywhere.
Investors were acting as though stocks could only move in one direction.
Up.
There is a famous story that Kennedy decided to sell his stocks after a shoeshine boy started giving him investment advice.
The lesson sounds perfect.
When absolutely everyone thinks they're a stock market expert, maybe the market is getting dangerously overheated.
There's just one problem.
Historians haven't found reliable contemporary evidence that this conversation actually happened.
It's a great story.
But great stories aren't always great history.
What is better supported is that Kennedy reduced much of his stock exposure before the crash.
He also profited from bets against declining stock prices.
Instead of being trapped in the collapse, he had positioned himself to survive it.
Think about the difference between Kennedy and an average investor.
The average investor might have had nearly all their savings tied up in the market.
They might have borrowed money to buy even more.
And when prices collapsed, they had very little flexibility.
Kennedy had already moved much of his wealth out of danger.
He had access to capital.
He had business experience.
And he had the financial flexibility to take advantage of opportunities after the crash.
This is where the story gets especially interesting.
Because avoiding a disaster is one way to protect your money.
But having money available after a disaster creates an entirely different advantage.
###
Imagine two people entering the Great Depression.
The first person owns stocks purchased with borrowed money.
The market collapses.
Their investments lose value, but their debts don't simply disappear.
They may be forced to sell assets at terrible prices.
Now imagine a second person.
They sold much of their risky investments before the collapse.
They aren't desperate to repay a broker.
And they still have substantial cash or other liquid assets available.
When businesses and property owners become desperate to sell, the second person has choices.
They can wait.
They can negotiate.
They can buy assets that other people can no longer afford to hold.
The first person is forced to react.
The second person has the luxury of deciding.
And during a financial crisis, that difference can be enormous.
But there's an important detail.
Cash wasn't completely safe either.
During the Great Depression, thousands of banks suspended operations.
Keeping all your savings in a failing bank could be disastrous.
So the real advantage wasn't simply holding cash.
It was maintaining financial flexibility while avoiding catastrophic losses.
Kennedy's wealth was not created entirely by the Depression.
He was already wealthy before the crash.
But his timing helped preserve and expand the fortune he had built.
His business interests continued to develop, and his family's wealth would eventually become the foundation of a political dynasty.
The Kennedy family entered the following decades with extraordinary financial resources.
But here's the uncomfortable reality.
For an ordinary family struggling to buy food, these opportunities were mostly theoretical.
It's easy to say, "Buy when everyone else is selling."
It's much harder when you've lost your job, your savings, and your ability to borrow.
A crisis does not magically create equal opportunities.
In many cases, it makes existing differences in financial resources even more important.
And that's one reason some fortunes survived while others disappeared.
But now we need to return to Jesse Livermore.
Because his story reveals the other side of the equation.
###
Remember Jesse Livermore?
The trader who became famous for making an extraordinary amount of money from the 1929 crash?
You might expect the rest of his life to be simple.
Retire.
Buy a mansion.
Enjoy the fortune.
Never look at another stock chart again.
Unfortunately, Livermore wasn't built that way.
Trading was more than a profession to him.
It was a lifelong obsession.
And winning one enormous trade did not mean he could never lose again.
After 1929, Livermore continued facing financial and personal difficulties.
He eventually lost the fortune that had made him famous.
In 1934, he filed for bankruptcy.
Think about that.
A man who reportedly earned around one hundred million dollars from one of history's most devastating market crashes was bankrupt only a few years later.
The complete financial details of his losses aren't fully documented.
But his career had always followed a dangerous pattern.
Extraordinary success.
Enormous risks.
Devastating losses.
Then another attempt to recover.
His later life was marked by serious personal struggles.
In 1940, Livermore died by suicide.
It was a tragic ending, and no single financial mistake can explain his life or death.
But his financial story does raise an important question.
What's the point of making a fortune if you can't protect it?
Livermore understood how to profit from collapsing markets.
But even that extraordinary skill did not guarantee long-term financial security.
Kennedy took a different path.
He preserved his wealth, developed business interests, and helped establish a lasting family fortune.
Both men navigated the same historic disaster.
But their long-term outcomes were dramatically different.
And that brings us to the real lesson of 1929.
###
When people hear stories about investors getting rich during the Great Depression, they usually imagine a secret trick.
Maybe someone knew the market would crash.
Maybe someone had the perfect investment strategy.
Maybe there was one magical asset that always went up.
But history is messier than that.
Some people made money by short selling.
Others protected themselves by reducing their exposure before the collapse.
Some wealthy investors were able to acquire assets during the difficult years that followed.
But many wealthy people also lost enormous amounts of money.
And millions of ordinary Americans had no realistic opportunity to turn the crisis into a profit.
The Great Depression wasn't a guaranteed money-making opportunity.
It was a catastrophe.
The people who benefited were exceptions, not the rule.
Still, their stories reveal three important differences between financial survival and financial destruction.
First, debt can turn a bad investment into a disaster.
When you borrow heavily to invest, a market crash doesn't just reduce your wealth.
It can eliminate your ability to recover.
Second, liquidity creates options.
Having financial resources available during a crisis can give you time to think and act instead of being forced into desperate decisions.
And third, making money is not the same as managing money.
Jesse Livermore was extraordinarily skilled at making speculative profits.
But even his greatest triumph couldn't guarantee that his fortune would last.
Joseph Kennedy's story highlights a different kind of financial advantage.
Preserving capital before a disaster can matter just as much as finding profitable opportunities afterward.
And perhaps that's the strangest truth about financial history.
A market crash doesn't affect everyone in the same way.
For some people, it destroys years of progress.
For others, it creates extraordinary opportunities.
Not because the disaster is good.
But because the same economic event can produce completely different outcomes depending on debt, timing, resources, and decisions.
In 1929, millions of Americans learned that lesson in the hardest possible way.
And nearly a century later, the question remains just as relevant.
When the next major financial crisis arrives, will you be forced to sell?
Or will you have the financial freedom to choose?
Because sometimes, the biggest difference between losing everything and surviving a crisis isn't predicting the future.
It's making sure one bad future can't destroy you.
